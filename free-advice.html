STEP 01 / ACQUIRE A LEAD

Get the right person
to raise their hand.

A lead is someone who has shown interest in a job you can do and given you a way to continue the conversation. A view, a like, or a number on a list is not the same thing.

What you’ll leave withA clear offer, a way to reach potential customers, three ready-to-edit scripts, and a place to track inquiries.
1. Choose the job2. Show your work3. Find your customers4. Start a conversation5. Capture the inquiryYour first hour
01

Choose one job you want more of.

“We do everything” makes it hard for someone to know when to call you. Pick a service you can deliver well, an area you can serve, and a customer who needs that job.

Fill in this sentence

We help [type of customer] in [area] with [specific job]. Contact us to [simple next step].

Example: We replace worn-out wood fences for homeowners in Courtenay and Comox. Send us a photo and your location to start a quote.

Only offer what you can actually deliver. Your next step should match your process: an initial phone conversation, photos to check fit, or a booked site visit.

02

Give them a reason to believe you.

Choose three clear photos of relevant work. Tell people what the customer needed and what you did. If you have a before-and-after, use both.

  • Name the service and general area.
  • Explain the work in a sentence or two.
  • Include genuine customer feedback if you have permission.
  • Make your contact information easy to find.

Starting out? Show your own relevant experience or clearly labelled sample work. Don’t present another contractor’s project as yours.

03

There is more than one way to get a lead.

Referrals, online discovery, paid advertising, and local visibility can all start a conversation. Build a trustworthy online presence, then choose channels that fit your service area, budget, and capacity. You do not need to launch everything at once.

Your online profile supports every channel.

A homeowner might see your truck, hear your name at a hockey game, receive your postcard, or get a referral from a friend—and then look you up before calling.

Give them a clear website, current contact information, real job photos, and honest customer reviews. A well-kept online presence is an asset you can keep building: each new project and review gives future customers more to judge you by.

Quick check: Search your business name on your phone. Is it obvious what you do, where you work, whether your work looks good, and how to reach you?

Customer referrals

People who already trust your work can introduce you to someone else who needs it. Be clear about the jobs and area you serve. Keep in touch with past customers when there is a relevant reason.

Try this: After completing a job, let the customer know you welcome introductions. Give them one easy link or contact card to pass along.

Check the result: Record who referred each inquiry. Track which referrals turn into suitable jobs; thank the person without pressuring the new customer.

Reactivating old quotes

A past inquiry may still turn into work. Some customers postponed the project, needed time to decide, or lost track of the quote. An old quote is a conversation to revisit—not a guaranteed job.

Try this: Review your open quotes and choose five appropriate contacts who have not declined or asked you to stop. Try: “Hey [name], are you still planning [project], or has that been put on hold?” Confirm scope, availability, and current pricing before accepting the job.

Check the result: Track replies, revised quotes, and booked work separately from brand-new inquiries. Update each status and stop following up when the customer says no.

Repeat customers and the next project

A customer who knows your work may need another project or a service that naturally follows the first one. Relevant maintenance, a second phase, or work at another property can create another opportunity.

Try this: Review completed jobs for a real reason to reconnect. Try: “Hey [name], how is [completed project] holding up? You mentioned [next project] when we last spoke—is that still something you’re considering?” Only refer to plans they actually mentioned.

Check the result: Track returning-customer inquiries and bookings. Keep the message useful and timely, and respect contact preferences.

Other trades and local business relationships

Build relationships with businesses whose customers need related work: landscapers, builders, property managers, painters, and other trades. Learn what they do before asking for referrals.

Try this: Choose three complementary businesses. Show relevant work, explain your service area, and ask which requests they regularly receive but cannot handle.

Check the result: Track partner introductions and booked jobs. Agree on any referral arrangement clearly rather than implying a partnership that does not exist.

Paid ads: Google, Facebook, and Instagram

Search ads can reach people looking for a service. Social ads put your work in front of people while they browse. Start with a specific job, relevant local area, real proof, and one clear next step.

Try this: Pick one service and one channel. Set a test budget you can afford, define a suitable inquiry, and send people to a matching page or short form. Check the form and response process before spending.

Check the result: Measure qualified inquiries, quotes, and booked jobs—not clicks alone. Set a spend limit and review results before increasing the budget. Include ad spend and management costs when comparing returns.

Organic social media: your own posts and reels

Your Facebook or Instagram page can show the work behind your business without paying to distribute every post. Share before-and-afters, short job walkthroughs, answers to common questions, and the people doing the work.

Try this: Turn one real project into a before-and-after post, a short video, and an answer to a customer question. Name the service area and explain how to inquire. Reuse useful footage rather than inventing something unrelated.

Check the result: Track inquiries and website visits from your content, not just views. Organic reach is not guaranteed, and useful local attention matters more than a large unrelated audience.

Google Business Profile, Google reviews, and organic search

Complete your eligible Google Business Profile with accurate services, contact details, hours, and service area. Add real photos. Your website should clearly explain the jobs you do and locations you actually serve.

Try this: Check your profile and website on a phone. Ask real customers for honest Google reviews using your review link, without incentives or requests for a particular rating. Reply professionally to feedback.

Check the result: Ask new inquiries how they found you. Keep profile information current; better information and reviews do not guarantee a top ranking. Google’s local ranking guidance ↗ · Google’s review guidance ↗

Yelp and relevant local directories

Make sure your listing accurately shows your services, area, contact details, and work. A directory is worth attention when people in your market actually use it to find your trade.

Try this: Find and check your business listing. Correct outdated information, add relevant project photos, and check where customer inquiries arrive.

Check the result: Track whether the directory brings suitable inquiries before paying for extra exposure. Yelp differs from Google: it tells businesses not to ask customers for Yelp reviews. Yelp’s review policy ↗

Local Facebook groups

Community groups are different from posting on your own business page: you are joining someone else’s space. Answer relevant requests and share local project proof where business promotion is allowed.

Try this: Read the group rules. Post a recent job with photos, the general area, a clear description, and one way to contact you. Use the community-post script below.

Check the result: Record inquiries from each group. Helpful replies and relevant posts are more useful than repeatedly dropping the same advertisement.

Door hangers and neighbourhood flyers

Reach nearby homeowners with a specific service and proof of work. A recent job in the neighbourhood can make your message relevant, with the customer’s permission if you mention or show their property.

Try this: Choose a small area that fits the work you want. Use a readable design with your service, a real project photo, business name, and one contact route. Respect no-soliciting signs and local distribution rules.

Check the result: Use a campaign-specific link, QR code, or “How did you hear about us?” field. Count printing and distribution time as costs.

Custom postcards and neighbourhood mail

Mail a useful, specific offer to selected neighbourhoods. Match the service to the properties: a fence-replacement message belongs in an area where that work makes sense.

Try this: Choose a manageable test area, get printing and postage quotes, and build one clear card. Canada Post Neighbourhood Mail and USPS Every Door Direct Mail provide route-based mailing options. Check eligible routes, formats, and delivery exclusions rather than assuming every household will receive it.

Check the result: Track the area, number mailed, campaign cost, inquiries, quotes, and booked jobs. Use a unique landing-page link or code. Canada Post Neighbourhood Mail ↗ · USPS EDDM ↗

Jobsite signs, branded vehicles, and billboards

Your name becomes easier to recognize when it appears consistently around the area you serve. Vehicle lettering, permitted job signs, and billboards can introduce your business before someone needs the work.

Try this: Use a readable business name, a short service description, and a memorable phone number or website. For a billboard, ask about location, audience, contract length, design costs, and the full placement cost.

Check the result: Treat broad visibility as a test, not a guaranteed stream of immediate leads. Ask callers where they first heard of you and what prompted them to contact you. Keep your online profile consistent with the sign.

For jobsite signs, get the property owner’s permission and use only locations where placement is allowed. Make the service and contact route readable at a glance; do not imply your sign represents another contractor’s work.

Hockey teams, baseball teams, and community sponsorships

Supporting a local team or event can put your name in front of families in your service area and build real relationships. Choose a community connection you are willing to support, not just a place to stick a logo.

Try this: Ask a team what the sponsorship includes: jerseys, rink or field signage, program space, announcements, or social posts. Confirm the season, deliverables, and total cost.

Check the result: Track introductions and inquiries where possible. Sponsorship can have community value even when direct sales are hard to attribute; do not budget as though every impression becomes a customer.

Local radio

A short, memorable message can introduce your service to listeners in your area. The job, service area, business name, and next step should be easy to understand without seeing anything.

Try this: Ask the station for audience geography, schedule, repetition, production costs, and the total package price. Use a short website address or memorable number and one clear service message.

Check the result: Give the campaign a defined budget and review point. Ask new callers whether they heard the ad and track suitable inquiries and bookings, not just estimated listener reach.

Property managers, strata managers, and HOA managers

Managers may coordinate maintenance and projects across multiple properties. Learn what they oversee, how contractors are selected, and who can approve the work.

Try this: Identify a few managers serving properties that fit your trade. Introduce your services, area, relevant project examples, and capacity. Ask about their vendor process and the documentation they require.

Check the result: Track vendor approval, invitations to quote, and awarded work. Being on a vendor list is not the same as having a booked job.

Builders and general contractors

Builders and general contractors may need dependable subcontractors for a particular part of a project. Be specific about the work you handle and the size of job your team can take on.

Try this: Choose local builders whose projects fit your services. Send a short capability summary with examples, service area, and contact details. Ask how they invite subcontractors to price upcoming work.

Check the result: Track invitations, quotes, and awarded projects. Confirm scope, schedule, payment terms, and responsibilities before committing.

Realtor relationships

A real estate agent may hear about cleanup, repairs, or improvements before a listing or after a purchase. Your work needs to fit the project and the customer’s timing.

Try this: Introduce yourself to agents working in your area. Show relevant examples and explain your actual availability. Ask what property-work requests they regularly need help with.

Check the result: Record agent introductions and bookings. Confirm who hires you and approves the quote; an introduction from an agent is not authorization to start work.

Supplier and rental-shop relationships

Building-supply yards, specialty suppliers, and rental shops sometimes hear from customers who need a contractor. A professional relationship helps them understand the work you do.

Try this: Get to know the businesses you already use. Ask whether they keep contractor cards, maintain a directory, or have a process for passing along contact details. Provide a concise description and permission to share your business contact information.

Check the result: Ask new customers who referred them. Do not imply a supplier endorses your business or promise referral fees without an agreement.

Home shows and community events

Events offer a chance to meet people planning projects and explain your work in person. Choose an event whose audience and location match the customers you want.

Try this: Before booking, check the total cost, likely audience, and what the booth package includes. Bring clear project photos and a simple inquiry form. Ask permission to follow up and record the project, location, and timing.

Check the result: Count suitable inquiries, site visits, quotes, and booked jobs. Include booth fees, materials, and staff time when judging whether the event was worthwhile.

Choose a starting mix.

Keep your website and profiles credible. Choose one active way to create conversations—such as referrals, a local post, or a small paid test. Track what happens before adding more channels.

When a lead comes in, the next job is the same: respond, check whether the project fits, and agree on the next step. Acquisition only pays when you can turn suitable inquiries into profitable work.

04

Make it easy to reply.

Use these as starting points. Replace every bracket with a real detail. Send personal messages only where you have an appropriate basis to contact the person, and respect requests to stop.

A past customer

Hey [name], hope you’re doing well. I’m taking on [type of work] around [area]. If you or someone you know is planning a project, feel free to pass along my details. Here’s a recent job: [link/photo]. — [your name], [business]

A local trade partner

Hey [name], I’m [your name] with [business]. We do [service] in [area]. Do your customers ever ask about [specific job] that you don’t handle? I’d be happy to show you our work and see whether it makes sense to keep in touch.

A permitted community post

Just finished this [project] in [area]. The customer needed [problem], so we [work completed]. [Attach your own photos.] If you’re planning something similar in [service area], send me your location and a quick description of the job. I’m [name] with [business].

05

Have a place for the inquiry to land.

Choose one main contact route: phone, email, or a short form. Test it yourself. Make sure someone is responsible for checking it and replying.

When someone replies

“Thanks for reaching out. What are you looking to have done, where is the job, and when are you hoping to start? A couple of photos would help too.”

Record their name, contact details, job location, service, timing, source, and your next action. Do not make someone fill out a long business questionnaire to request a fence quote.

FREE GUIDE

Follow up without chasing forever

A simple way to keep open quotes moving.

01 Make one list

Track the customer, job, quote amount, date sent, last contact, next action, and current status. A spreadsheet is enough to start. Keep customer information in a private file.

02 Agree on the next contact

When you send the quote, ask when they expect to decide and agree on a useful time to check in. Follow that timing instead of sending reminders blindly.

03 Send a helpful question

“Hey [name], checking you received the quote for [job]. Is there anything you want me to clarify before you decide?” Keep the message about their project.

04 Know when to stop

Mark the quote won, lost, postponed, or awaiting a reply. Stop automated follow-up when they respond, book, decline, or ask you to stop. Check applicable messaging rules before automating.

05 Your next step

Review your open quotes. Choose five where a follow-up is appropriate, give each a next action, and log the outcome.

FREE GUIDE

Make your website easier to buy from

Five checks you can do on your phone.

01 Say what you do and where

Your opening should explain the service and service area. “Fence installation and repairs in [city]” gives a visitor useful information immediately.

02 Show real work

Use your own project photos and explain what you did. Add customer feedback you have permission to use. Keep claims specific and accurate.

03 Give people a clear next step

Use a visible call button and a short inquiry form. Ask for contact information, job location, and a brief description. You can gather detailed scope later.

04 Test the whole inquiry

Open the site on your phone. Tap the number and send a test form. Check that the message arrives and someone knows who will reply.

05 Your next step

Ask someone unfamiliar with your business to look at the opening for five seconds. Can they tell you what you do, where you work, and how to contact you?

FREE GUIDE

Check the job before you send the price

A practical estimate checklist, not a universal markup.

01 List the direct costs

Include materials, crew time, your own labour, equipment, rentals, delivery, disposal, and subcontractors. Use supplier quotes and realistic hours for this job.

02 Account for the business costs

Insurance, vehicles, software, admin time, and other overhead still need to be covered. Use your actual costs and expected workload when choosing an allocation method.

03 Separate markup from margin

For illustration: a job costing $1,000 sold for $1,250 has a 25% markup and a 20% margin before any costs you left out. They are different calculations, not interchangeable targets.

04 Write the assumptions down

State what is included, what is excluded, and what happens if the scope changes. Access, ground conditions, disposal, and customer changes can affect the job.

05 Your next step

Compare one finished job’s estimated labour, materials, and other costs with what actually happened. Use the difference to improve your next estimate.

FREE GUIDE

Find out which leads are worth paying for

Start with a small, useful scorecard.

01 Record the source

Ask how the customer found you. Use consistent categories such as referral, Google profile, search ad, or social media. “Not sure” is better than a guess.

02 Track the outcome

Count inquiries, quotes sent, and booked jobs by source. Compare a consistent period and allow time for open quotes to reach a decision.

03 Compare spend with booked work

For illustration: $600 of advertising that produces three booked jobs has an advertising cost of $200 per booked job. That is not profit, and it excludes other selling costs.

04 Look at job quality too

Consider project fit, travel, available capacity, and actual profit. A large number of inquiries can still mean poor business if the jobs do not fit.

05 Your next step

Add a lead-source field to your list and review it weekly. Use the data to decide what to improve before raising your advertising budget.
